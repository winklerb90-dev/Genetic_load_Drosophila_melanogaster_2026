# Genetic load in _Drosophila Melanogaster_, 2026
Internship on the difference in impact of genetic load on fitness between male and female *Drosophila melanogaster*.\
Written at Johannes Gutenberg-University Mainz, under the supervision of Dr. Thomas Keaney, by Benjamin Winkler.\
\
**Brief background on theory:**\
The theory is given by [Charlesworth et al., 1987](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2461884?seq=1) and [Rice, 1984](https://academic.oup.com/evolut/article-abstract/38/4/735/6872210?redirectedFrom=fulltext):\
In short, Charlesworth et al. (1987) showed that when selection acts on both sexes (homogametic (XX), heterogametic sex (XY)) and dosage
compensation exists, a new beneficial allele will fix relatively faster on the X chromosome compared to an autosome if the allele is sufficiently
recessive (Eq. 2a). This has been labelled the *faster-X* effect. Reversely, they found that when selection acts on both sexes or only the males and dosage compensation exists, a new  deleterious allele will fix relatively slower on the X chromosome compared to an autosome if the allele is recessive enough (Eq. 8a, 9). Previously to these findings, Rice (1984) examined the invasion of a sexually antagonistic allele and found that the more recessive an allele is
the more does the advantage to the heterogametic sex outweighs the disadvantage to the homogametic sex (Eq. 3). X-linked loci are always exposed in males, but can be recessive and 'hidden' to selection in females. This fact as well as the above studies suggest that perhaps the X chromosome is purified from male-deleterious loci but accumulates recessive female-deleterious loci. This is the key idea that we wanted to test, using the methods which are outlined below.\
\
**Brief method summary:**\
To precise the idea, we wanted to examine the relationship between the accumulation of deleterious variants and fitness, and if differences exist which can be traced back to chromosome type and sex. To make this an even comparison we used genotypes from the *Drosophila Genetic Reference Panel*, created by [Mackay et al., 2012](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10811). The DGRP features genotypes from 205 heavily inbred and nearly homozygous *Drosophila melanogaster* lines, giving us data in which the   effects of deleterious recessives may have become exposed. To identify putatively deleterious variants, we used two independent methods: First via the estimated function and impact of variants, using [SnpEff](https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff/) by [Cingolani et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3679285/). And secondly via the calculation of rejected substitutions across the *D. melanogaster* genome, using GERP++ by [Davydov  et al., 2010](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1001025). This data was then matched with annotations and fitness essays generated by [Wong & Holman, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37738246/), to generate the genetic load per chromosome, a measurement of the realised and potential mutational burden caused by deleterious variants, as described by [Chen et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-025-02802-8). We then investigated the relationship between genetic load and fitness using the [brms](https://paulbuerkner.com/brms/) package by [Bürkner, 2018](https://www.jstatsoft.org/article/view/v080i01).\
We report the detailed methods in this repository.
